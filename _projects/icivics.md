## The problem

iCivics teaches civics to millions of students, and many of its lesson readings were PDFs. Teachers need to adapt readings for different reading levels, languages, and supports, and a static PDF doesn't let them. It wasn't accessible, couldn't be adjusted, offered no language options, and gave teachers no way to assign work digitally, save student work, or grade it.

## What I did

I interviewed 10 teachers and stakeholders, then sorted what I heard on an affinity map. Five needs emerged: **accessibility, language support, interactive tools, reading-level leveling, and customization**.

{% include figure.html src="/assets/img/icivics/synthesis.webp" alt="An affinity map of green sticky notes from the interviews, grouped into columns for accessibility, language, tools, leveling, customization, and PDF pain points." width="1600" height="900" caption="Synthesizing 10 interviews into five needs." %}

I turned the interviews into three teacher personas, each centered on one of those needs, in teachers' own words:

- **Customization:** "I want to be able to use the material that I want and cut out the rest."
- **Accessibility:** "Addressing accessibility is helpful for all students, not just those with special needs."
- **Leveling:** "The goal isn't to 'dumb' down the material, but rather build the student up."

Then I reviewed five platforms teachers already used (CommonLit, The DBQ Project, Newsela, Achieve3000, and Kami) and mapped how each one handled the five needs, from read-aloud audio and adjustable fonts to multilingual translation, guided questions, and reading-level selectors.

## Outcome

I proposed a first iteration of an iCivics digital reader built around three features that met the most pressing needs: **annotation tools**, **read-aloud voiceover**, and **captions for images**.

{% include figure.html src="/assets/img/icivics/first-iteration.webp" alt="Slide titled First iteration for iCivics, listing annotation tools, read-aloud voiceover, and captions for images, next to a low-fidelity wireframe of an article page with a toolbar." width="1600" height="900" caption="The first-iteration concept." %}

One question from the research stayed with me: if a reader can adjust a text's level, who should decide, the teacher or the student? Leveling can build a student up, or quietly hold them back.
