## The problem

Most students we surveyed wanted to spend less time on Instagram and TikTok, and most had already tried. Of 109 students, **84% had tried to cut back**, usually by setting app time limits, deleting apps, or turning off notifications. And **75% said they'd ideally spend less than an hour a day**, while most were spending one to four hours. The biggest barrier wasn't a lack of willpower so much as habit: 70% named **habitual checking**, ahead of fear of missing out and the need for social connection.

## The idea

NOMO, short for "No Missing Out," turns cutting back into a group challenge. Friends join a challenge together, such as spending less than an hour a day on social media, and if the whole group succeeds, NOMO plants a tree. The survey shaped that reward: most students were motivated by productivity, time management, and mental health, and an environmental nonprofit was their top choice of cause.

## What I did

I helped run the survey and analyzed the results, which showed what kind of accountability students actually wanted:

{% include nomo-results.html %}

Then I ran usability tests of the prototype with 10 students and reviewed the onboarding copy line by line. Testing pointed to clear fixes:

- **Say what the app does up front.** Several testers couldn't tell what they were signing up for from the first screen.
- **Make joining obvious.** Testers expected "Join Challenge" to show a list of challenges, and didn't think to enter a friend's code. Label it "Join with a code."
- **Offer solo and shorter challenges,** so people can try NOMO before committing a whole friend group.
- **Clarify progress and status.** The progress bar confused testers, and "expired" read as "failed."
- **Soften the accountability.** An early version revealed to the group who broke the challenge, and my very first prototype even had a "Top Destroyers" leaderboard. Feedback called for a gentler tone, matching the survey: accountability, without an audience.

## Outcome

In prototype tests, the new rewards and streak mechanics lifted **click-through by 22%** and **retention by 16%**.

NOMO has since launched. Today, people earn "Moments" for scrolling less and trade them for rewards like sports and concert tickets, merch, and cash back, which echoes the survey, where event tickets were students' second-favorite reward. They can also challenge friends, or bump phones with someone in person to lock social media for 30 or 60 minutes.

The project left me with a question I'm still thinking about. NOMO uses the same tools as the apps it competes with, like streaks, rewards, and social pressure, to pull people away from them. Students told us they wanted accountability but not exposure. Where is the line between a nudge that supports someone and one that just keeps them hooked?
